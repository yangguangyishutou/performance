\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Model Signature and Watermark}
The commercial value and immense training costs of deep learning models have established them as critical intellectual property (IP), prompting extensive research into model watermarking and provenance tracking. Early defense mechanisms primarily targeted discriminative neural networks to thwart model extraction and surrogate attacks \cite{zhang2024robust, zhu2024reliable, gan2023towards}. These methods established ownership by verifying embedded external features or backdoor triggers in a black-box setting \cite{li2022defending, li2023black}. More proactive defenses, such as Bayesian active watermarking, have since been developed to actively inject traceability during user queries, mitigating the risk of unauthorized model cloning across various paradigms, including federated learning \cite{wang2024defense, charette2022cosine, yang2024fedgmark}.

As the AI landscape shifted towards generative architectures, adversaries began exploiting unauthorized fine-tuning and task adaptation to bypass traditional IP protections. Consequently, visual and multi-modal watermarking evolved to embed robust, structural fingerprints that survive downstream model modifications. To comprehensively protect these diverse generative assets, researchers have embedded invisible, verifiable signatures across a wide spectrum of generation processes, encompassing latent and pixel-space diffusion models \cite{yang2024gaussian, fernandez2023stable, guo2024freqmark, wang2024achieving, wen2023tree, huang2024robin, liu2023mirror}, as well as emerging audio and video synthesis frameworks \cite{hu2025videoshield, liu2024groot, zong2025audiomarknet, ji2025speech}. Moving beyond mere generation tracking to combat model adaptation, techniques like SleeperMark \cite{wang2025sleepermark} explicitly disentangle watermark information from semantic concepts in text-to-image diffusion models, ensuring the model retains its embedded fingerprint even after substantial downstream fine-tuning. Similar structural resilience has been integrated into diverse architectures, spanning GANs \cite{lin2024cyclegan, fei2024wide}, 3D scene representations like NeRFs and 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{luo2025nerf, jang20253d, chen2025guardsplat}, and pivotal tuning processes for pre-trained image generators \cite{lukas2023ptw}. However, the robustness of these continuous-domain watermarks is continually challenged by universal forgery and erasure attacks \cite{muller2025black, kassis2025unmarker}.

In the domain of Large Language Models (LLMs), watermarking initially emerged as a mechanism to detect machine-generated text via vocabulary partitioning, typically by promoting a randomized ``green list'' of tokens during decoding \cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. Subsequent research heavily optimized these foundational methods to enhance text quality, information capacity, and statistical robustness \cite{fu2024gumbelsoft, fu2024watermarking, wu2023resilient, wang2023towards, yoo2023robust, liu2024adaptive, huo2024token, chen2025improved, hou2024k, ren2024subtle, ji2024principled, qiang2023natural}, alongside advanced algorithms for segmenting and detecting partial watermarks \cite{li2024segmenting, zhao2025efficiently}. Concurrently, adversaries have developed sophisticated evasion techniques, including color-aware substitution, paraphrasing, and cross-lingual attacks \cite{wang2024stumbling, wu2024bypassing, he2024can, jovanovic2024watermark}. However, as the primary threat landscape evolved from content misuse to intellectual property theft via knowledge distillation, researchers pivoted to adapt these text-tracking techniques for proprietary model protection. Building upon the statistical manipulation of decoding probabilities, specialized black-box frameworks such as PLMmark \cite{li2023plmmark} and GINSEW \cite{zhao2023protecting} were developed to detect unauthorized student models that inherit the behavioral fingerprints of the protected teacher model. 

Beyond simple binary detection, securing model provenance requires addressing critical cryptographic vulnerabilities such as key leakage and malicious framing. To this end, researchers have introduced unforgeable, publicly verifiable watermarking schemes (UPV) that utilize dual neural networks to decouple the generation and detection keys \cite{liu2023unforgeable}. Furthermore, defending against spoofing attacks--where adversaries forge harmful content to misattribute blame to the model owner--has driven the development of bi-level signature schemes like Bileve \cite{zhou2024bileve}. Advancing from model-level to user-level provenance, recent frameworks have also enabled multi-user watermarking, allowing developers to trace unauthorized model extraction or data leakage back to individual colluding users even under adaptive prompting \cite{cohen2025watermarking}. Collectively, these advancements provide a robust mechanism to trace model lineage and detect unauthorized distillation across the AI supply chain \cite{sander2024watermarking, pan2025can, pang2025modelshield}. To achieve holistic ecosystem protection, tracking mechanisms are also being aggressively extended to safeguard peripheral, high-value assets such as training data, source code, and prompts \cite{sun2023codemark, yang2024srcmarker, yao2024promptcare}. These dynamic arms races are comprehensively evaluated in recent benchmarks and surveys \cite{tu2024waterbench, zhao2025sok, zhong2023copyright, salman2023raising}.

\subsection{Model Fingerprinting and Similarity}
Beyond active watermarking, model fingerprinting seeks to identify the unique, inherent characteristics of models to track their provenance and verify ownership. These techniques span a spectrum from black-box behavioral profiling to white-box static analysis. In black-box scenarios, fingerprinting relies on analyzing a model's dynamic inference behavior in response to carefully crafted inputs. For discriminative models, this involves analyzing output correlation statistics on specific samples or learning the subspace of universal adversarial perturbations \cite{guan2022you, peng2022fingerprinting, yang2022metafinger}. As Large Language Models (LLMs) became prevalent, these dynamic probing techniques evolved to handle complex deployments. Researchers developed methods to actively query integrated applications to identify the underlying LLM engine \cite{pasquini2025llmmap}, or constructed specific prompts which often use adversarial suffixes or dual-layered stylistic and semantic triggers to reliably elicit unique behavioral signatures even after the model has undergone fine-tuning or merging \cite{gubri2024trap, yamabe2025mergeprint, xu2026dnf}. Furthermore, analyzing the output distributions and log-likelihood vectors across standardized task suites allows for the construction of functional model DNA or phylogenetic maps, enabling the clustering and lineage tracking of hundreds of black-box LLMs \cite{wu2025llm, yax2024phylolm, oyama2025mapping}.

Transitioning to white-box settings, researchers exploit access to internal model states to derive highly robust fingerprints without relying solely on black-box output distributions. Initial white-box approaches evaluated the similarity of internal representations, gradient tensors, and activation differences to establish familial links \cite{zhang2024reef, wu2025tensorguard, xu2025delta}. Moving towards completely static offline analysis, recent advancements delve entirely into the parameter space, extracting intrinsic signatures directly from the model weights. These purely static analyses include leveraging the statistical distributions of attention parameters \cite{yoon2025intrinsic}, identifying directionally invariant terms within the weights to generate verifiable ownership proofs \cite{zeng2024huref}, and utilizing singular value decomposition to extract invariant spectral signatures from attention matrices for lineage tracking \cite{wang2025ghost}. Furthermore, rigorous statistical methods evaluating weight matrix pairs have been introduced to specifically detect unauthorized weight copying and plagiarism \cite{zhang2025matrix}. Building upon these offline parameter measurements, unsupervised algorithms have also been proposed to reconstruct precise evolutionary model trees, a process achieved by formulating lineage recovery as a directed minimum spanning tree problem based on the $L_2$ distances between model weights \cite{horwitz2024unsupervised}.

\subsection{Layer-wise Dynamics in Fine-tuning}
Understanding the impact of fine-tuning on pre-trained language models requires examining both macroscopic representation spaces and microscopic parametric mechanisms. At the macroscopic level, fine-tuning adjusts layer-wise hidden states to efficiently extract task-specific features without catastrophically altering the foundational semantic capabilities of early layers \cite{van2019does}. This adaptation alters the global embedding geometry primarily by expanding inter-class margins while preserving the original spatial topology of the pre-trained data \cite{zhou2022closer}. Microscopically, these representation shifts are governed by specific structural modules, particularly feed-forward networks (FFNs) acting as hierarchical key-value memories \cite{geva2021transformer}. The adaptation process within these layers is highly localized; modifying distinct FFN sub-layers yields divergent knowledge updating dynamics \cite{qiu2024empirical}, and specific facts are often sparsely encoded in distinct ``knowledge neurons'' that allow for targeted interventions \cite{dai2022knowledge}. Ultimately, the layer-wise adaptation during fine-tuning exhibits an intrinsic rank-deficiency, demonstrating that these complex weight updates can be robustly approximated by injecting trainable low-rank matrices into frozen Transformer layers \cite{hu2022lora}. These layer-wise insights provide the theoretical foundation for tracing model lineage during downstream adaptation.

\subsection{Model Lineage Analysis}
Building upon model fingerprinting and pairwise similarity techniques, recent research has advanced towards systematically reconstructing the evolutionary lineage of neural networks. While earlier methods primarily focused on calculating undirected distances or pair-wise similarities--such as using centered kernel alignment \cite{zhang2024reef}, gradient-based fingerprints \cite{wu2025tensorguard}, matrix-level homology \cite{zhang2025matrix}, or log-likelihood vectors \cite{oyama2025mapping}--to cluster models or detect unauthorized derivations, the current frontier emphasizes the construction of directed lineage trees and graphs. For instance, to map the opaque landscape of fine-tuned and distilled models, researchers have adapted phylogenetic algorithms and defined low-dimensional functional ``LLM DNA'' based on model outputs to build evolutionary trees and dendrograms \cite{wu2025llm, yax2024phylolm}. In the white-box domain, neural lineage detection has been formalized to explicitly predict parent-child tuning relationships \cite{yu2024neural}, and unsupervised algorithms have been designed to decode directed minimal spanning trees from model weights by leveraging weight kurtosis as a proxy for evolutionary direction \cite{horwitz2024unsupervised}. Ultimately, these methodologies converge toward the broader vision of a ``Model Atlas'', a unified graph charting the developmental trajectories and weight transformations of the global model population, often utilizing release metadata and timestamps to anchor temporal lineage \cite{horwitz2025we}.

\subsection{Software and LLM Supply Chain}


Researchers have extensively studied the security and trust challenges in open-source software supply chains. 
Boughton~\cite{boughton2024decomposing} propose a framework for decomposing and measuring trust in open-source software supply chains by identifying factors that influence the trustworthiness of dependencies. From an adversarial perspective, Ladisa et al.\cite{ladisa2023sok} provide a systematic overview of attack techniques targeting open-source software supply chains. Complementary to these studies, Zahan et al.~\cite{zahan2023software} evaluate security metrics and practices in open-source dependencies. Focusing on the JavaScript ecosystem, Zahan~\cite{zahan2022weak} analyze npm package metadata and propose weak-link signals to detect potential supply chain risks, while Miller~\cite{miller2025understanding} study how the npm ecosystem responds to dependency abandonment and its implications for supply chain sustainability. With the rapid growth of deep learning development, researchers have increasingly focused on the deep learning software supply chain. Tan et al.\cite{tan2022exploratory, gao2024characterizing} and Chen et al.\cite{chen2025understanding} conduct exploratory studies on the supply chains of major deep learning frameworks (e.g., PyTorch and TensorFlow) to understand their downstream dependencies, development dynamics, collaboration patterns, and ecosystem differences. From another perspective, some studies focus on faults and failures in the deep learning stack. Huang et al.\cite{huang2023demystifying} perform an empirical investigation of dependency-related bugs in the deep learning software stack. Jajal et al.\cite{jajal2024interoperability} examine interoperability issues of ONNX model converters in deep learning frameworks, highlighting the challenges of cross-framework model portability.


Software Composition Analysis is widely leveraged to systematically identify software components, supporting tasks such as vulnerability detection~\cite{zhao2023software, jiang2024binaryai}, license analysis~\cite{german2009license}, and supply chain risk assessment~\cite{Nocera2025SoftwareCA}. The Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)~\cite{yu2024correctness, sehgal2023taxonomy, mirakhorli2024landscape} provides a structured description of the internal components of a software system and their dependency relationships. Traditional SCA techniques focus on extracting components from artifacts at different levels. For example, explicit dependencies can be parsed from build configuration files (e.g., package.json and pom.xml)~\cite{zimmermann2019small, huang2022characterizing}. Static code analysis can be used to identify calls to third-party libraries~\cite{gao2024characterizing}. Binary feature matching can detect repackaged components~\cite{yang2022modx}. With the rise of LLMs, the composition analysis has expanded beyond traditional elements to include models, datasets, frameworks, prompts, and external tools invoked during execution~\cite{wang2025large, huang2024lifting}. 
The concept of SBOM has been extended to the AI domain, such as AI Bill of Materials (AI BOM)~\cite{aibom, bennet2025implementing} and ML Bill of Materials (ML BOM)~\cite{mlbom}. At the standardization level, CycloneDX has extended support for ML BOM, and SPDX 3.0 has also begun to discuss and support the modeling of AI-related assets~\cite{spdx}. However, achieving transparent composition analysis for LLM-based systems presents substantial technical challenges. Prior studies have found that such models may memorize training data and leak parts of the training corpus~\cite{song2019auditing, carlini2021extracting}. To infer training data membership, membership inference attacks (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership} have been adopted.
Mattern et al.~\cite{mattern2023membership} propose generating contrastive samples that are semantically similar to the target sample to amplify the sample differences. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024pretraining} infer membership by measuring the deviation between the word frequency distribution within a document and that of the overall corpus. 
Shi et al.b~\cite{shi2023detecting} and Li et al.~\cite{li2025uncovering} propose the reference-free approaches based on the hypothesis that low-probability tokens tend to appear less frequently in member samples. 
Nevertheless, Duan et al.~\cite{duan2024membership} found that the boundary between member and non-member samples is inherently fuzzy and continuous in the context of LLMs, leading to limited effectiveness. Besides, Ren et al.~\cite{ren2025demystifying} studies the evolution of neural networks by constructing a large-scale neural network bill of materials database.
