\noindent\textbf{Model Attribution and Similarity Metrics.} 
Determining the provenance of Large Language Models (LLMs) traditionally relies on watermarking, which identifies ownership from outputs but sacrifices generation quality~\cite{kirchenbauer2024watermarklargelanguagemodels,yang2023watermarkingtextgeneratedblackbox,christ2023undetectablewatermarkslanguagemodels,kuditipudi2024robustdistortionfreewatermarkslanguage}. Alternatively, active model fingerprinting injects specific triggers via lightweight tuning~\cite{fingerprintingdiffusionmodel,xu-etal-2024-instructional,russinovich2025heythatsmodelintroducing}. However, these intrusive modifications risk performance degradation and exhibit poor robustness against complex fine-tuning~\cite{nasery2025scalablefingerprintinglargelanguage}. Consequently, recent research has shifted toward post-hoc similarity metrics, which evaluate model relationships without prior injection. These approaches are broadly categorized into dynamic inference and static analysis. Dynamic methods evaluate pairwise similarity during runtime, operating either as black-box approaches comparing output distributions~\cite{pasquinillmmapfingerprintinglargelanguage} or white-box approaches computing activation similarities (e.g., CKA)~\cite{zhang2024reefrepresentationencodingfingerprints} and gradient statistics~\cite{wu2025gradientbasedmodelfingerprintingllm}. However, dynamic inference necessitates extensive computational resources and forward-pass overhead. This significant cost has driven a transition toward static analysis, which directly examines white-box weights. While initial static approaches utilize cross-layer statistical consistencies~\cite{yoon2025intrinsicfingerprintllmscontinue} or Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)~\cite{wang2025ghosttransformerdetectingmodel}, they can be vulnerable to weight manipulations. To robustly determine whether two models are related even under adversarial permutation and scaling attacks, state-of-the-art static methods focus on computing structural invariants~\cite{huref}, often integrating SVD to extract these robust architectural signatures~\cite{wu2026llmdna}. Despite their efficacy, all aforementioned metrics evaluate similarity strictly in a pairwise manner.

\noindent\textbf{Ecosystem-Scale Model Lineage Reconstruction.} 
Beyond pairwise verification, pioneering works scale similarity metrics to reconstruct macroscopic evolutionary graphs of the open-source ecosystem, aiming to uncover complex $N$-to-$N$ genealogical trees~\cite{rahman2025hugginggraphunderstandingsupplychain}. Initial black-box methods leverage dynamic inference to compute vocabulary probability differences, such as Nei's genetic distance, for phylogenetic tree construction~\cite{yax2025phylolminferringphylogeny}. However, relying purely on distance yields unrooted trees that fail to infer evolutionary directionality and often misidentify root models. To resolve this lack of directionality, recent white-box approaches incorporate intrinsic or extrinsic signals. Specifically, they utilize weight-based $L_2$ distances combined with layer kurtosis~\cite{horwitz2025unsupervised} or metadata timestamps~\cite{horwitz2025charting} to construct directed lineages, accurately charting how foundation models adapt and branch over time.

\noindent\textbf{Layer-wise Dynamics in Transformers.}
Understanding the layer-wise internal mechanisms of Transformers has long been a focal point for model interpretability and architecture optimization. Early studies analyzed how linguistic features and reasoning processes evolve across hidden layers~\cite{van_Aken_2019}, and demonstrated the benefits of layer-by-layer coordination for learning hidden representations~\cite{layerwisecoordinationbetweenencoderdecoder}. More recently, the focus has shifted toward comparing inter-model and intra-model dynamics, such as tracing the progressive progression of representational similarity across layers~\cite{TracingRepresentationProgression} and tracking attention-aware relevance propagation to faithfully understand model behavior~\cite{achtibat2024attnlrpattentionawarelayerwiserelevance}. In the context of model provenance, emerging evidence suggests that specific parameter blocks do not evolve equally; for instance, attention weight matrices have been shown to exhibit highly invariant spectral signatures during fine-tuning and model reuse~\cite{wang2025ghosttransformerdetectingmodel}. Despite these mechanistic insights, existing lineage detection frameworks largely treat all transformer parameters homogeneously when calculating inter-model distances. It remains unexplored how individual components—namely the Q, K, V, and MLP matrices—differentially contribute to the accuracy of macroscopic lineage graph reconstruction. Our empirical study (RQ2) bridges this gap by systematically isolating and quantifying the evolutionary significance of each transformer block component.
