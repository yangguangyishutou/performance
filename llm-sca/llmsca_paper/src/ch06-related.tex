\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Model Identity Representation}

\textbf{Model watermarking} deliberately embedded the triggers into the model to protect its intellectual property. Early mechanisms primarily targeted neural networks to defend against model extraction and surrogate attacks~\cite{zhang2024robust, zhu2024reliable, gan2023towards, li2022defending, li2023black, wang2024defense, charette2022cosine}. Subsequent research has focused on embedding invisible and verifiable signatures into generative models~\cite{yang2024gaussian, fernandez2023stable, wen2023tree, huang2024robin, liu2023mirror, hu2025videoshield, liu2024groot, zong2025audiomarknet} to protect AI-generated multimedia content, including images~\cite{wang2024achieving}, videos~\cite{hu2025videoshield}, and audio~\cite{zong2025audiomarknet, ji2025speech}. Meanwhile, several studies aim to experiment watermark robustness against downstream fine-tuning~\cite{wang2025sleepermark} universal attacks~\cite{muller2025black}, and forgery attacks~\cite{muller2025black}.
Watermarking for LLMs was initially introduced to detect machine-generated text by biasing token selection during decoding~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. Subsequent work has improved in terms of text quality, embedding capacity, and fine-grained detection capabilities~\cite{fu2024gumbelsoft, fu2024watermarking, wu2023resilient, wang2023towards, yoo2023robust, liu2024adaptive, huo2024token, chen2025improved, hou2024k, ren2024subtle, ji2024principled, qiang2023natural, li2024segmenting, zhao2025efficiently}. Meanwhile, adversarial studies have demonstrated evasion via paraphrasing, substitution, and cross-lingual transformations~\cite{wang2024stumbling, wu2024bypassing, he2024can, jovanovic2024watermark}. 
In addition, recent approaches address challenges such as unforgeability~\cite{liu2023unforgeable} and support multi-user watermarking schemes~\cite{cohen2025watermarking}. Beyond model-level protection, emerging work extends watermarking techniques to data~\cite{pan2025can}, text~\cite{zhou2024bileve}, code~\cite{sun2023codemark, yang2024srcmarker}, and prompts~\cite{yao2024promptcare}.

\textbf{Model fingerprinting} aims to build distinctive identifiers of models, encompassing both black-box behavioral profiling and white-box static analysis. Black-box fingerprinting relies on analyzing a model's dynamic inference behavior in response to carefully crafted inputs~\cite{guan2022you, peng2022fingerprinting, yang2022metafinger}, including modeling models' outputs as unique vector subspaces~\cite{pasquini2025llmmap, oyama2025mapping},  functional representations~\cite{wu2025llm}, constructed prompts as adversarial suffixes~\cite{gubri2024trap, yamabe2025mergeprint, xu2026dnf}. White-box fingerprinting, in contrast, leverages access to internal model states to derive distinctive model profiles. Dynamic white-box approaches capture runtime characteristics, including feature representations~\cite{zhang2024reef}, gradient tensors~\cite{wu2025tensorguard}, and activation differences~\cite{xu2025delta}. Static white-box fingerprinting captures intrinsic weight characteristics, including statistical distributions~\cite{yoon2025intrinsic} (e.g., $L_2$ distances of model tensors~\cite{horwitz2024unsupervised}), directionally invariant terms~\cite{zeng2024huref}, SVD-based spectral signatures~\cite{wang2025ghost}, and matrix-driven homology proofs~\cite{zhang2025matrix}.

\subsection{Model Relation Analysis}

Building upon model fingerprinting techniques, recent research has advanced towards reconstructing the model relationships. Using dynamic white-box fingerprints, Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025tensorguard} proposes TensorGuard, which is a gradient-based fingerprinting to detect LLM lineage and classifies model families by analyzing layer-wise gradient responses to input perturbations. Besides, Oyama et al.~\cite{oyama2025mapping} construct a model map using log-likelihood vectors from over 1,000 models. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025llm} introduces LLM DNA, a theoretically grounded, low-dimensional representation of model behavior. Using static fingerprints, Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024reef} compares the pairwise feature representations of the two models using centered kernel alignment while remaining robust to model modifications like fine-tuning, pruning, and merging. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2025matrix} proposes to detects and reconstructs weight correspondences using matrix analysis and statistical testing for LLM plagiarism detection without requiring inference. Based on fingerprinting and similarity analysis, recent research has begun to explicitly model LLM lineage. For instance, Yax et al.\cite{yax2024phylolm} apply phylogenetic algorithms to LLMs by leveraging output-based similarity to construct evolutionary trees that capture model relationships. Complementing this perspective, Yu et al.\cite{yu2024neural} propose neural lineage detection methods, including both learning-free and learning-based approaches, to identify parent and ancestral relationships among models. Shifting from behavioral to structural analysis, Horwitz~\cite{horwitz2024unsupervised} introduces MoTHer, which reconstructs lineage trees by analyzing neural network weights and inferring parent-child relationships via a directed minimum spanning tree. Specifically regarding graph construction, Horwitz et al.~\cite{horwitz2024unsupervised} build a Model Tree by calculating pairwise weight distances to identify topological connections and utilizing weight kurtosis monotonicity to infer edge directions, ultimately recovering the structure via a minimum directed spanning tree (MDST) algorithm~\cite{chu1965shortest,edmonds1967optimum}. Expanding this to a global scale, their Model Atlas framework~\cite{horwitz2025we} constructs a non-tree Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) where nodes represent models and edges denote weight transformations. To chart unknown regions of this graph, they employ a greedy algorithm guided by real-world structural priors. This process involves leveraging temporal upload dynamics to determine edge directionality, treating quantized models as leaf nodes, and explicitly accommodating multi-parent merged models to map complex relationships across massive model repositories.

\subsection{Software and LLM Supply Chain}


Researchers have extensively studied the security and trust challenges in open-source software supply chains. Boughton~\cite{boughton2024decomposing} propose a framework for decomposing and measuring trust in open-source software supply chains by identifying factors that influence the trustworthiness of dependencies. From an adversarial perspective, Ladisa et al.\cite{ladisa2023sok} provide a systematic overview of attack techniques targeting open-source software supply chains. Complementary to these studies, Zahan et al.~\cite{zahan2023software} evaluate security metrics and practices in open-source dependencies. Focusing on the JavaScript ecosystem, Zahan~\cite{zahan2022weak} analyze npm package metadata and propose weak-link signals to detect potential supply chain risks, while Miller~\cite{miller2025understanding} study how the npm ecosystem responds to dependency abandonment and its implications for supply chain sustainability. With the rapid growth of deep learning development, researchers have increasingly focused on the deep learning software supply chain. Tan et al.\cite{tan2022exploratory, gao2024characterizing} and Chen et al.\cite{chen2025understanding} conduct exploratory studies on the supply chains of major deep learning frameworks (e.g., PyTorch and TensorFlow) to understand their downstream dependencies, development dynamics, collaboration patterns, and ecosystem differences. From another perspective, some studies focus on faults and failures in the deep learning stack. Huang et al.\cite{huang2023demystifying} perform an empirical investigation of dependency-related bugs in the deep learning software stack. Jajal et al.\cite{jajal2024interoperability} examine interoperability issues of ONNX model converters in deep learning frameworks, highlighting the challenges of cross-framework model portability.


Software composition analysis is widely leveraged to systematically identify software components, supporting tasks such as vulnerability detection~\cite{zhao2023software, jiang2024binaryai}, license analysis~\cite{german2009license}, and supply chain risk assessment~\cite{Nocera2025SoftwareCA}. The Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)~\cite{yu2024correctness, sehgal2023taxonomy, mirakhorli2024landscape} provides a structured description of the internal components of a software system and their dependency relationships. Traditional SCA techniques focus on extracting components from artifacts at different levels. For example, explicit dependencies can be parsed from build configuration files (e.g., package.json and pom.xml)~\cite{zimmermann2019small, huang2022characterizing}. Static code analysis can be used to identify calls to third-party libraries~\cite{gao2024characterizing}. Binary feature matching can detect repackaged components~\cite{yang2022modx}. With the rise of LLMs, the composition analysis has expanded beyond traditional elements to include models, datasets, frameworks, prompts, and external tools invoked during execution~\cite{wang2025large, huang2024lifting}. 
The concept of SBOM has been extended to the AI domain, such as AI Bill of Materials (AI BOM)~\cite{aibom, bennet2025implementing} and ML Bill of Materials (ML BOM)~\cite{mlbom}. At the standardization level, CycloneDX has extended support for ML BOM, and SPDX 3.0 has also begun to discuss and support the modeling of AI-related assets~\cite{spdx}. However, achieving transparent composition analysis for LLM-based systems presents substantial technical challenges. Prior studies have found that such models may memorize training data and leak parts of the training corpus~\cite{song2019auditing, carlini2021extracting}. To infer training data membership, membership inference attacks (MIA)~\cite{shokri2017membership} have been adopted.
Mattern et al.~\cite{mattern2023membership} propose generating contrastive samples that are semantically similar to the target sample to amplify the sample differences. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024pretraining} infer membership by measuring the deviation between the word frequency distribution within a document and that of the overall corpus. 
Shi et al.b~\cite{shi2023detecting} and Li et al.~\cite{li2025uncovering} propose the reference-free approaches based on the hypothesis that low-probability tokens tend to appear less frequently in member samples. 
Nevertheless, Duan et al.~\cite{duan2024membership} found that the boundary between member and non-member samples is inherently fuzzy and continuous in the context of LLMs, leading to limited effectiveness. Besides, Ren et al.~\cite{ren2025demystifying} studies the evolution of neural networks by constructing a large-scale neural network bill of materials database.
